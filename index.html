Public Service: From Research to Implementation.

I am building a career around a simple question: how can institutions serve people more responsibly—not only in policy, but in the ordinary moments when policy becomes experience?

Why public service

A desire to serve became a discipline to prepare.

Growing up, I saw how addiction, economic hardship, justice-system involvement, and institutional indifference can narrow people’s choices. I learned early that vulnerability is often compounded—or eased—by the quality of the systems around it.

Those experiences did not give me easy answers or authority over other people’s stories. They gave me a responsibility to prepare carefully before claiming I could improve the systems that shape their lives.

Over time, the desire to serve became a discipline: learn the institutions, test assumptions against evidence, understand how rules are implemented, and notice how people experience them.

A guiding idea

Public responsibility begins before the title.

I hope, over time, to serve in elected office. Before asking for that responsibility, I want to keep becoming more useful in the work that precedes it: listening, researching, organizing, explaining, and following through.

Learn → test → serve → reflect

Learning the systems

Studying institutions from the inside and out.

At UT Austin, economics and government taught me to ask how incentives, institutions, and political behavior shape outcomes. At Georgetown, American Government brought those questions closer to implementation—through teaching, campaign work, reentry services, and a qualitative capstone on frontline governance.

Wanting to help was the beginning. Learning how institutions distribute power, opportunity, and responsibility became the work.

A foundation in direct service

Long before I studied institutions, I was learning how to serve people directly.

Disability support

Special Angels of The Woodlands & Best Buddies

Beginning in high school, I supported people with physical and cognitive disabilities through Special Angels of The Woodlands and Best Buddies. Those relationships taught me to value inclusion, dignity, patience, and the everyday work of helping people participate fully.

Hospitality · 2021–2025

Pappasito’s Cantina

Across four years in service, to-go operations, bartending, training, and headwaiting, I learned to anticipate needs, communicate across backgrounds, resolve problems under pressure, and take responsibility for a team’s shared standard.

That hospitality experience later helped me welcome prospective students and families at PJI. Public service is also experienced through tone, preparation, and whether someone feels respected when they walk through the door.

Research taught me to verify. Teaching taught me to explain. Campaigning taught me to listen.

Reentry in practice

Georgetown’s Prisons and Justice Initiative · January 2026 – August 2026

Reentry Assistant — Operations & Program Support

At PJI, research, education, and operations met people in real time. I supported prospective and current participants and family members, helped route reentry questions and resources, maintained records and program materials, and coordinated with staff, instructors, and community partners.

My work extended across the office and correctional education settings: technology and classroom logistics at the D.C. Jail, research support for students at Patuxent, Paralegal Program exam coordination, career and family programming, external outreach, and the ordinary handoffs that help someone leave with a clearer path forward.

I also built practical infrastructure—inventory and location guides, repeatable supply-count systems, document workflows, shared-space organization, and a later operations guide prepared for staff review. Some inventory work remained in progress when the role ended, and the public portfolio does not expose internal procedures or participant information.

Questions carried to the page

Research with practical purpose.

My research asks how institutions exercise discretion, shape behavior, and translate policy into lived experience. These briefs make each project’s question, method, evidence, and limits visible without asking a reader to begin with a 20- or 59-page paper.

Featured flagship Qualitative case study

Reentry governance · Embedded research

Our Sacred Space: Governance of Reentry at Georgetown’s Prisons and Justice Initiative

A case study of PJI—the organization where I was working while conducting the research—using nine recorded interviews, 15 field journals, participant observation, and document analysis. Sustained presence inside the organization was influenced in part by Richard Fenno’s emphasis on learning through observation and informal interaction; the project was not a replication of his congressional research. The full paper remains private because it contains sensitive internal material.
